🟢 97 Things Every Software Architect Should Know: Business topics

##17 Business drives

An architect functions as an intermediary between business and technologies. Technology must not be an end in itself, it is only a tool that must primarily serve to fulfill business goals.

##30 Understand the business domain

For effective architecture design, you must deeply understand the field (domain) in which the company operates. Without domain knowledge, you will design a system that does not solve real customer needs.

##64 The ROI variable

Every architectural decision is a form of investment (cost). Success is measured by return on investment (ROI). The benefit does not have to be directly financial, risk mitigation or time savings also count. Example: Investing 4 hours in tests shortens subsequent bugfixing from 10 to 2 hours (saving 8 hours = 200% ROI).

##85 Make a strong business case

To push through a technical proposal with management, you need 5 steps:

  1. Value: Focus on increasing business productivity and efficiency, not on how great the given technology is.
  2. Metrics: Determine how to realistically detect and measure improvement.
  3. Business indicators: Use metrics understandable to management. Beware of Goodhart's Law ("When a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a good metric"). Do not tie KPIs to financial rewards, rather consider OKR.
  4. Roadmap: Prepare milestones linked to business value and define "emergency exits" from the project.
  5. Timing: The right moment is key. When "the house is on fire" (crisis situation), proposals for systemic improvements often pass more easily.

##87 Learn a new language

Learn to speak the "language of business". An architect must be able to translate purely technical IT problems (e.g., solving NullPointerException or refactoring over-complex design) into understandable arguments for management (risk reduction, stability, savings on future development costs).

##92 Pay down your technical debt

Shortcuts in development for the purpose of quick delivery ("quick and dirty") generate debt. The interest is growing instability and more expensive maintenance. This debt must be continuously paid off (refactor code), otherwise the codebase becomes unmaintainable.

#Goodhart's Law

"When a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a good metric".

As soon as you tell people that their evaluation and money depend on one specific number, they will do everything to meet it. Even at the cost of damaging the system itself. People do not optimize their work for the good of the company, but to meet the given metric. Therefore, the book warns against tying KPIs to financial incentives.

Example from development: If you tie rewards to the number of resolved tickets, developers will start artificially breaking tasks into smaller pieces, choosing only trivial ones and ignoring complex architectural problems. KPIs are green, but the product stagnates.

#OKR (Objectives and Key Results)

A modern alternative to rigid KPIs. It has two clearly separated parts:

Objective: Qualitative, ambitious and inspirational direction (e.g., "Radically speed up deployment to production").

Key Results: 2 to 5 quantitative metrics that measure progress (e.g., "Shorten build time below 5 minutes", "Achieve 90% test coverage for new code").

Why it works and how it bypasses Goodhart's Law:

Separation from money: Fulfilling OKR is not standardly tied to calculating personal bonuses. It serves to set direction and motivation, not to punish. Thus, the need to manipulate statistics disappears.

Context: Metrics (Key Results) do not stand alone as with KPIs, but are always tied to a higher purpose (Objective). If the team finds that a given metric does not lead to the goal, they simply change it in the next quarter.

Support for ambitions: In OKR, 70% fulfillment is often considered an excellent result. People are not afraid to set "moonshot" goals because they know that they will not lose part of their salary for not achieving 100%. The goal is to push boundaries, not play it safe to fulfill an Excel table.

#Example from practice: Integration of HW sensor into old C module

  • #30 Understand the business domain: You understand that the company is not about academically clean API. It is about the business's ability to quickly churn out support for new peripherals and meet delivery deadlines.
  • #17 Business drives: You do not deploy hexagonal architecture for engineering joy. You do it because core isolation from hardware is the only tool to shorten time-to-market and allow the team to work in parallel.
  • #87 Learn a new language: You do not tell management "we will remove tight coupling and inject dependencies". You say: "Communication isolation will allow us to meet the key release and significantly reduce the risk of system crashes directly at the customer's site."
  • #64 The ROI variable & #92 Pay down technical debt: You present refactoring as an investment with clear return: "Current technical debt extends integration to 3 weeks. Investing 14 days in rewrite (debt repayment) will shorten future integrations to 3 days. Return on investment (ROI) occurs immediately after deploying the second sensor."
  • #85 Make a strong business case (Avoiding Goodhart's Law): You do not propose rigid KPIs like "Rewrite 80% of old header files". Developers would start moving code just to meet percentages. Instead, you propose value-focused OKR:
    • Objective: Make HW integration routine.
    • Key Result: Reduce average lead time for adding peripherals from 21 to 5 days.
