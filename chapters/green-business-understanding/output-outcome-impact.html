🟢 Output vs. Outcome vs. Impact

#Summary / Introduction

The three concepts Output – Outcome – Impact describe the chain of value created during software development: from a tangible delivery, through a change in customer behavior, to a measurable impact on the business. Distinguishing between these levels is essential for directing effort correctly, because delivery itself (output) has no value in its own right – value is created only by the result (outcome), which produces an impact (impact). The topic is addressed in particular by the book Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble, Kim, 2018) and the associated DORA model. For architecture and testing, the topic is important because it determines what should actually be measured and optimized: not the amount of code produced, but the real change that the software brings about for the customer and in the business.

Source / additional material: Video – Accelerate: Output vs. Outcome

#Definitions of the Concepts (Using an Example from Medicine)

An example from medicine is used for illustration, clearly separating the individual levels.

Concept Medical Example Meaning in IT Measurability
Output (Output) The operation itself (cutting, suturing) Tangible code, a specific delivery, release, implementation Easy to measure
Outcome (Customer Outcome) The patient survives and recovers A change in user behavior, customer satisfaction, realization of a benefit Harder to measure
Impact (Business Impact) The patient returns to work, the hospital's revenue increases Market share, profit, total revenue Easy to measure, metrics available
  • Output (Output): The lowest level of the chain. It is the activity itself and its tangible product. It is easy to quantify (the number of story points, lines of code, releases), and that is precisely why it is tempting to mistakenly consider it a measure of success.
  • Outcome (Customer Outcome): The change that the output brings about for the customer – different behavior, a solved problem, a benefit gained. It is harder to measure, but it is what truly carries value.
  • Impact (Business Impact): The aggregate impact of the outcomes at the organizational or market level. It is again readily measurable using business metrics.

#The Trap of Perfect Output

  • An operation can be technically perfect, yet the patient still dies. The same applies in IT: perfect output combined with a negative outcome means failure.
  • Success (“The Win”) depends exclusively on a positive outcome.
  • Output is merely a means (a tool) to achieve a result, not a goal in itself.

An important conclusion follows: measuring and rewarding only the volume of output leads to what is known as a “feature factory,” where a team produces many features without anyone verifying their actual benefit.

#The Golden Rule

  • Code is expensive to maintain, so it makes no sense to celebrate only a large volume of completed work.
  • The general rule is: output should be minimized while outcome is maximized.
  • In other words, the ideal is to achieve the greatest possible change for the customer with the smallest possible amount of code (the least costly solution).

#The Role of the Architect and Continuous Delivery

  • According to the book Accelerate, architects should focus primarily on engineers and “outcomes,” not on specific tools or technologies.
  • There is a statistically proven way to improve outcome: improving Continuous Delivery (continuous delivery).

#The DORA Model (Connection to Continuous Delivery)

The chain Output → Outcome → Impact is directly related to the DORA model, which describes how a team's capabilities lead through delivery performance to outcomes:

Capabilities → (Software delivery) performance → Outcomes

  • Capabilities (capabilities): climate for learning (an environment for learning), fast flow (rapid flow), fast feedback (rapid feedback).
  • Software delivery performance (key metrics):
    • Change lead time (time from a change to deployment),
    • Deployment frequency (frequency of deployment),
    • Change fail percentage (proportion of unsuccessful changes),
    • Recovery time (time to recover after a failed deployment).
  • Outcomes (results):
    • Organizational performance (commercial and non-commercial organizational performance),
    • Well-being (job satisfaction, productivity, reduced burnout).

This model shows that outcome (and consequently impact) is not a matter of chance, but of the capabilities and performance of the delivery process, which can be measured and improved.

#Common Mistakes and Anti-patterns

Mistake / Anti-pattern Why It Is a Problem How It Manifests How to Prevent It
Measuring success by output volume Activity is rewarded, not value A “feature factory,” many unused features Track outcome (a change in behavior, a benefit)
Confusing output with outcome Leads to a false sense of success Delivery is “complete,” but the customer is dissatisfied Define the target outcome before delivery
Ignoring impact The connection to the business is lost Features without a measurable benefit Link outcome to business metrics
Optimizing tools instead of results The architect addresses technologies, not value Discussions about tooling without a connection to the goal Focus on engineers and outcomes (Accelerate)
Maximizing output Maintenance costs increase Bloated code, technical debt Minimize output, maximize outcome

#Practical Example: Perfect Output, Tragic Outcome (Unification of a Security Component)

Situation (Playing Field and Forces) An existing component was being upgraded to a new secure version. We decided to replace fragmented proprietary variants with a single unified one. In the Decision Group (representatives of all clients), the proposal and the architecture were jointly approved, including the need to write future adapter classes.

Output (Maximized) The output was a technically perfect product. Compilable code wrapped up with a bow, connected to CI/CD, tested, with generated documentation and sample adapter classes. From an engineering perspective, a 100% success.

Outcome (Minimized) Clients refused to deploy it or gradually dropped out. They discovered that implementing real, context-dependent adapter classes required too much effort in practice. They preferred to continue developing their own proprietary solutions, which they trusted. The customer gained no real benefit, did not start using the component, and the development team's effort was wasted.

Failure Analysis (Reality vs. Theory)

  • False Walking Skeleton (Illusion of Agreement): During the review, the clients approved an empty skeleton (headers and API declarations), which cost them nothing. They did not perform a trial implementation. A real Skeleton would only have been tested once a client had actually wired the API into their code (perhaps using mocked data) and felt the integration pain before the rest of the system was written.
  • GPD without a Shared Business Goal: The Decision Group did technically approve the solution (Decision), but a strong shared business objective (Goal) was missing. "Unification" is a goal for the IT department, not for the clients. If a stakeholder does not see an immediate advantage for their product, integration is just an unnecessary cost to them.
  • Triumph of Conway's Law (Non-technical Forces): In the company, independent business owners hold the real power, and senior management is afraid to exert pressure centrally. The architecture inevitably adapted to the company's structure: the attempt to create a central component came up against decentralized management and logically failed.

#Examination Questions

Basic Level

  • Explain the difference between output, outcome, and impact. → A definition of all three levels and their measurability is expected, ideally using the example from medicine.
  • What carries real value? → Outcome, not output.

Intermediate Level

  • What is the “trap of perfect output”? → Perfect output with a negative outcome = failure.
  • What is the golden rule of the output/outcome relationship? → Minimize output, maximize outcome, and explain why (maintenance costs).

Advanced Level

  • How does Output/Outcome/Impact relate to the DORA model? → Capabilities → performance → outcomes, key metrics.
  • According to Accelerate, what should an architect focus on? → Engineers and outcomes, not tools and technologies.

Questions about Practical Experience

  • Give an example where good output did not lead to a good outcome. → A specific experience and lessons learned are expected.

Questions about Decisions and Trade-offs

  • How can pressure for rapid deliveries (output) and actual benefit (outcome) be balanced? → Argue in terms of measuring outcome and maintenance costs.
  • Why is measuring only impact insufficient? → Impact is distant and influenced by many factors; outcome is closer and more controllable.

#Model Answer (3–5 Minutes)

The concepts of output, outcome, and impact describe the chain through which value is created in software development. Output is a tangible delivery – specific code, a release, or an implementation. It is easy to measure, and it is therefore tempting to consider it a measure of success. Outcome is a change in customer behavior, customer satisfaction, or a benefit achieved; it is harder to measure, but it is precisely what truly carries value. Impact is the aggregate effect on the business – market share, profit, revenue – and is again readily measurable.

The difference between them is well illustrated by an example from medicine: output is the operation itself, outcome is the patient's recovery, and impact is the patient's return to work and the increase in the hospital's revenue. The key lesson is that an operation can be technically perfect, yet the patient still dies. Applied to IT: good output with a negative outcome means failure. Success depends exclusively on a positive outcome; output is only a means of achieving it.

The golden rule follows: output should be minimized and outcome maximized. Code is expensive to maintain, so there is no point in celebrating the mere volume of work. The ideal is the greatest possible change for the customer with the smallest possible amount of code.

This perspective is directly connected to the architect's role. According to the book Accelerate, architects should focus on engineers and outcomes, not on specific tools and technologies. A statistically proven way to improve outcome is to improve Continuous Delivery. This is described by the DORA model, according to which a team's capabilities lead through delivery performance to outcomes, including both organizational performance and people's satisfaction.

From the perspective of testing and quality, the distinction is important because it determines what should be verified in the first place: not only whether a feature works (output), but whether it actually brought about the intended change (outcome). The trade-off lies in the pressure for rapid, visible deliveries versus measuring the actual benefit, which is slower and harder to capture.

In summary: value is created not by the amount of software produced, but by the change it brings about – which is why outcome, not output, is optimized.

  1. Output = delivery, Outcome = change for the customer, Impact = effect on the business.
  2. Outcome carries value, not output.
  3. Good output + negative outcome = failure.
  4. Golden rule: minimize output, maximize outcome.
  5. Better outcome is achieved through Continuous Delivery (DORA/Accelerate).

5 Key Concepts
