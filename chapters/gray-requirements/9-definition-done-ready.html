Definition of Done (DoD) vs. Definition of Ready (DoR)

Most agile teams know and use Definition of Done (DoD) to verify whether work on a User Story is complete and the product is ready for deployment. Definition of Ready (DoR) stands at the opposite end of the cycle – it acts as an entry gate. It checks the quality of the requirements and tasks that the development team takes over from the Product Owner before starting work on them.

The following two concepts are used to ensure a smooth development flow and to clarify boundaries:

  • DoR (Definition of Ready): The conditions a task (e.g., a User Story) must meet before it is allowed into the development sprint. (E.g., it has defined acceptance criteria, the SWA has reviewed the architectural impacts, the story satisfies INVEST.)
  • DoD (Definition of Done): A checklist defining what "done" means. It applies to all tasks (e.g., code is written, has passed code review, unit tests have 80% coverage, architecture documentation is updated.)
  • Acceptance Criteria: These are specific to the individual User Story (whereas DoD is global for the entire project/team).

#Main benefits of introducing DoR

  • Smoother and faster Sprint Planning: The team does not have to waste planning time uncovering basic ambiguities in the specification.

  • Preventing unnecessary Rework: If a developer has the right information from the outset, the risk of development heading in the wrong direction is dramatically reduced.

  • Reducing Requirements Churn: Situations in which a task's specification changes fundamentally during a sprint already in progress are minimized.

  • Greater commitment and quality: A clear specification strengthens the team's confidence that it can successfully complete the work, which positively affects the resulting code quality.

#Main benefits of introducing DoD

  • Shared understanding of quality (Eliminating ambiguity): The team gains a uniform, objective, and shared view of exactly what the word "done" means, definitively eliminating subjective interpretations and statements such as "it works on my machine".

  • Continuous prevention of technical debt (Ensuring internal quality): Regular and strict fulfilment of technical criteria (such as code review, refactoring, or static analysis) ensures that the system's internal quality remains high and that the code does not become cluttered over time.

  • Reducing risks before release (Shift-Left approach): Integrating testing, security checks, and deployment processes directly into every sprint prevents critical errors and incompatibilities from appearing only shortly before the planned product release.

  • Greater transparency for the business (Stakeholder trust): Once a backlog item is marked as "Done", the Product Owner and management have absolute certainty that the code meets all of the organization's production, legislative, and architectural standards without hidden unfinished work.

  • Team stability and predictability (Velocity): Strict adherence to the criteria minimizes tasks being returned from QA to developers (the so-called boomerang effect), stabilizing delivery speed and ensuring a smoother Flow of work.
