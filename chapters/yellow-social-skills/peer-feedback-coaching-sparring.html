🟡 Peer feedback, coaching and sparring

#Introduction: Why these concepts belong together

  • Feedback as step zero: For effective sparring to even begin, you must be able to safely name that something is not working. The Perception-Effect-Wish model serves this purpose. Without mastered feedback, you cannot objectively define the problem and the discussion degenerates into defense.
  • T-shaped profile for sparring: A narrow specialist (I-shaped) has a tendency to immediately advise and slide into "solution mode" (functions as a mentor). A "T-shaped" person has the necessary communication overlap and can guide the other through the process using questions (functions as a coach/sparring partner).
  • Generally: I-shaped vs. T-shaped
    • I-shaped: Narrow expert with deep knowledge of one domain, without overlap. Suitable for Complicated world (plan, BDUF, good practice).
    • T-shaped: Deep expertise + broad overview and soft skills across disciplines. Suitable for Complex world (agile, emergent practice).
  • Rapport the art of connecting with a colleague and creating psychological safety. Rapport is not just an isolated technique, but a communication bridge that allows you through mirroring (pacing) to safely guide the other person to their own solution (leading).
  • Pacing Technique for building rapport based on conscious reflection of partner's behavior. Non-verbally, you adapt to their posture, gestures, facial expression and breathing. Verbally, you take over their key words, sentence structure and preferred sensory channels. You can also consider overall needs and dressing style. It is not about blind imitation, but about natural tuning that subconsciously creates safety and trust.

#Peer Feedback

Foundation of growth and problem solving.

  • Perception: Describe facts, what you saw/heard, without evaluation.
  • Effect: Say how it affected you.
  • Wish: Formulate what you want differently next time.
  • Receiving: Do not defend yourself, ask for details to understand and thank.

#Why use sparring (Effort vs. Time)

  • Lone Hero: The "lone hero" approach, which solves everything alone, leads to constant and unsustainable growth of expended effort over time.
  • Peer Sparring: Using a sparring partner helps flatten and stabilize the effort curve over time, because you do not get stuck in a loop and find a more efficient way to the solution.

#Mentoring vs. Coaching

  • Mentoring: Advises, gives ready solutions (pushes knowledge).
  • Coaching: Listens, asks and guides you to come up with the solution yourself (pulls solution from you).

#Peer Sparring

  • Goal and KPI: Problem owner finds the solution themselves.
  • Partner role: Mirror, change perspective, maintain conversation structure.
  • Rules: Open questions, endure silence, absolute ban on "solution mode" (do not advise).
  • Types of questions: (examples)
    • Circular
      • how would a colleague describe the problem?
      • what do others think about it?
      • how would someone you respect behave in this situation?
    • Miracle
      • if it somehow resolved itself, how would you know?
      • how would you change it if you had the power?
      • if obstacles disappeared, what would you do as the first step?
    • Past successes
      • how did you handle a similar situation in the past?
      • what helped you most at that time?
    • Paradoxical (Getting things worse)
      • what would have to happen for it to be even worse?

#Conversation management models

4C (Meeting structure):

  1. Contact: Creating trust and psychological safety. Active listening.
  2. Contract: Agreement on meeting goal and time. What is the sparring goal, time frame, what we end with.
  3. Coaching: Questioning and active listening phase. Open questions, mirroring, different perspective.
  4. Consequences: What next? Commitment to action and deadline.

#SCORE (Questioning structure, how to frame sparring process)

  • Symptoms: What is not working now?
  • Causes: Where is the root/cause?
  • Outcomes: What should the target state look like?
  • Resources: What do you need to solve it (time, people, competencies)?
  • Effects: What will it really bring in terms of long-term benefit?

#Example from practice: Mentoring (Transition from Python to C/C++)

Situation: A developer with experience only in Python was to learn the basics and thinking in procedural C language (with the goal of understanding and using gRPC).

Approach: A combination of explanation and assigning homework, which was then discussed together. Result: after half a year a motivated and enthusiastic C++ programmer.

Application of theory:

  • This is pure mentoring, not sparring. The "pushing knowledge" approach was exactly what was needed at this stage of learning.
  • KAWA model: You purposefully supplemented components Knowing (what to do) and Able (how to do it, hard skills).
  • Motivation: The colleague had internal motivation (Willing) solved. By providing him with missing skills, an ideal win-win situation was created.

#Example from practice: Peer sparring and futile refactoring (Junior Architect)

Situation: A junior architect was tasked with proposing refactoring of a complex, historical and untouchable part of the framework. Management (decision makers) wanted quick successes and did not want long tedious work. Developers with huge autonomy put up strong passive resistance, saw no purpose, were used to safe routine (bugfixes) and were overall burned out. The architect tried workshops over Conceptboard to unify requirements, but only encountered reasons why it cannot be done.

Approach: You served as a sparring partner. You did not offer ready solutions, you tried to make him come up with next steps himself, and you created a safe environment for him to share frustration. Eventually the architect moved to another agile team to work on another form of refactoring.

Application of theory:

  • Limits of sparring: Even though you conducted sparring correctly (ban on "solution mode"), you hit a wall. Sparring does not solve a problem that lies completely outside the problem owner's zone of influence.
  • KAWA model:
    • Allowed (Circumstances): The architect had a toxic assignment – responsibility for delivery, but zero authority and real support to push it through people with veto rights.
    • Willing (Willingness): At developers was at absolute zero.
  • Motivation 3.0: The team had extreme Autonomy (management did not manage them), but they completely lacked Purpose. They had long ago drowned Mastery in operations and fear of past fails.
  • Selling an Architecture: This is an exact example of failure in "selling" architecture. There was a lack of clear definition of impacts and benefits (Effects from the SCORE model) and especially a strong sponsor from management who would provide the business case and force the team to change priorities. Ending the effort and moving to another project was a necessary change of circumstances (Allowed) to save the architect.
