🟡 Motivation Theory

#Types of motivation

  • Extrinsic (External): Carrot and stick (rewards and punishments). Requires constant external pressure, otherwise it disappears.
  • Intrinsic (Internal): Comes from within a person. You cannot artificially "create" it, you can only prepare an environment that supports it.
  • Motivation 3.0 (Dan Pink): Modern internal motivation stands on 3 pillars:

    • Autonomy (I manage my work myself),

    • Mastery (I improve in what I do),

    • Purpose (I know why I do it and it has an impact).

      Dan Pink's TED talk

      • "hard candle problem" - requires creative thinking, reusing of the box
      • "easy candle problem" - obvious solution
      • repeatedly tested and verified by experiment:
        • for routine, mechanical problems, higher reward has a positive result
        • for problems and situations that require creative and unconventional thinking, broader perspective, etc., higher reward value has a negative effect on the result (non-intuitive!)
      • for the 21st century and uncertain, non-linear times (VUCA - Volatile, Uncertain, Complex, Ambiguous), it is necessary to focus on internal motivation and its 3 main pillars.
      • Evidence: "Fedex Days" in Atlassian, 20% in Google, Encarta versus Wikipedia

#KAWA (Problem diagnosis)

The KAWA acronym is used to reveal the real cause when someone is not delivering results or appears demotivated. You filter through 4 areas:

  • K - Knowing (Knowledge): Do they know exactly what is expected of them? Do they know the goal and understand why it is important?

    Solution: Provide necessary information, set SMART goals and clearly explain the purpose.

  • A - Able (Ability): Do they know how to do the task? Do they have the necessary skills and experience?

    Solution: Help them improve skills (training, mentoring), or find someone with appropriate skill.

  • W - Willing (Willingness): Are they motivated for the work? What specifically would motivate them?

    Solution: Find out their personal goals and motivators, try to create a win-win situation.

  • A - Allowed (Possibilities/Circumstances): Do current conditions and environment even allow them to complete the task (do they have time, tools, authority, are they not overloaded by other tasks)?

    Solution: Check if conditions can be adjusted and remove external obstacles from the way.

#How to motivate (Process for leaders)

Assumption: The person has enough money not to worry about survival.

  1. Give feedback and express your wish (I want to find out the causes).
  2. Ask about reasons and turn on active listening.
  3. Pass the problem through the KAWA filter.
  4. Check levels of Autonomy, Mastery and Purpose. Let them come up with the solution themselves (this protects their Autonomy, when you come up with it, autonomy suffers).
  5. Agree on specific next steps.

#Internal motivators

  • Acceptance: People around me approve of what I do and who I am.
  • Status: My position is good and recognized by people I work with.
  • Order: There are enough rules and principles for a stable environment.
  • Relatedness: I have good social contacts with people at work and around it.
  • Power: I have enough space to influence what happens around me.
  • Curiosity: I have many things to explore and think about.
  • Goal: My life goal is reflected in the work I do.
  • Honor: I am proud that my personal values are reflected in how I work.
  • Mastery: My work challenges my abilities, but is still within my power.
  • Freedom: I am independent of others in my own work and responsibility.

#Moving Motivators (Exercise)

Tool for mapping internal motivation and impact of changes.

  1. A person arranges cards with motivators from least important to most important.
  2. A specific change is simulated (e.g., transition to architect role). Cards that the change strengthens move up, those that suffer go down. You immediately see whether the change charges or drains the person overall.
  3. Results are shared in the team. Knowledge of what drives colleagues forward radically improves cooperation and relationships.

#Hypothetical example from manager practice

Situation: You have an experienced developer (Peter) in the team who has visibly slowed down in the last month, is not delivering and is annoyed at standups. A month ago he was put in charge of refactoring an old, critical, but boring component.

1. Diagnosis (KAWA): You sit down with him for 1:1. You find out that he understands the assignment (knows WHAT), has the skills for it (knows HOW). But he has no idea why it is being done now, when other things are burning business-wise (does not know WHY). So the problem partly lies in communicating purpose, but partly it is already demotivation.

2. Application of theory (Motivation 3.0): He has good money, it's not that. You apply active listening and find out the state of his 3 pillars:

  • Purpose: At zero. Does not understand the benefit of work.
  • Mastery: Suffering. Digging in legacy code and learning nothing new.
  • Autonomy: Good, no one bothers him in the code.

3. Intervention and Moving Motivators: You explain the Purpose to him – the refactoring is necessary because next quarter's client integration depends on it. You do a quick Moving Motivators exercise. You find out that Peter has "Curiosity/Learning new things" motivator in first place. That is now completely down because of legacy code.

4. Solution (Let him come up with it): You do not come up with a solution for him (it would threaten disruption of his autonomy). You ask: "Peter, we have to do the refactoring. How can you set it up so that it is more fun for you and you fulfill your need to try new things?" Peter proposes himself that he will not just fix the component, but try to rewrite it using a new pattern he wanted to learn for a long time. Result: Goal is met (component will be clean) and Peter's Mastery and Autonomy shoot up.

#Practice: Honza (Senior, burnout and departure)

Diagnosis through KAWA:

  • Knowing / Able: No problem, top senior.
  • Allowed (Circumstances): Total block. Extreme delivery pressure limited his space to pay off technical debt. At the same time, zero personal capacity due to reconstruction and insomnia (circumstances outside work that affect performance).
  • Willing (Willingness): Motivation dropped to zero.

Breakdown according to Motivation 3.0 (Dan Pink):

  • Autonomy: Disrupted. He had no authority to solve technical debt, business dictated pace. AI vision was pushed on him by management with which he disagreed.
  • Mastery: Could not do things properly (limitation due to debt).
  • Purpose: Here came a fatal break. He lost purpose in saving the old project. The new goal (AI) did not bring purpose because upper management's conviction did not correspond to reality – Honza saw that people did not want it. The change of motivators drained him, you did not find a win-win situation.

#Practice: Dalibor (Junior expert, demotivation and over-engineering)

Diagnosis through KAWA:

  • Knowing: Did not know what was exactly expected. He thought complexity = good code. Team self-organization and coding guidelines gave him boundaries.
  • Able: Hard skills great, soft skills and interaction were missing. Solved by your mentoring.
  • Allowed: You opened space for him to grow beyond routine.
  • Willing: He needed challenges, otherwise he was bored.

Breakdown according to Motivation 3.0 (Dan Pink):

  • Autonomy: Supported by the team agreeing on guidelines themselves (you did not order it from above).
  • Mastery: Great shift of attention. Instead of unwanted over-engineering of routine things, he got space to become a master in RFID and innovation. At the same time, he advanced in soft skills.
  • Purpose: He understood that complex code harms the team. He directed his expertise where it has real impact (strategic innovation group, AI). He feels useful, socializes and is satisfied.
