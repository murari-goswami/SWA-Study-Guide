🟡 4C Learning Concept (Accelerated Learning)

The 4C model serves to accelerate the learning process and shorten the time between acquiring new knowledge and its application in practice. It is based on how the human brain naturally learns and applies these principles to increase the performance of agile teams.

#C1 - Connections

People do not learn with an empty head. They must connect the new topic with what they already know, what they want to learn, and also must connect with each other and create a safe environment.

Agile parallel: Aligning the team with project goals and vision. Sharing experiences of individual members and building psychological safety for open discussion.

#C2 - Concepts

Passive watching of PowerPoint presentations does not work. The brain receives information through multiple senses – listening, discussion, writing or reflection. People should share in pairs what they know, and the group validates it with each other.

Agile parallel: Breaking down vision into smaller parts (features/user stories) on cards that the whole team actively discusses, and regular daily synchronization (standups).

#C3 - Concrete Practice

The gap between understanding the concept and its real application must be closed as quickly as possible through active training. People learn by doing it or by teaching it to others ("when we teach, we learn").

Agile parallel: Learning the SCRUM framework by the team actually applying it to the project, or sharing knowledge through pair programming (extreme programming).

#C4 - Conclusions

The hardest phase of learning is retaining information. The conclusion forces participants to summarize what they learned, evaluate it and create a personal action plan for application in real life. Writing helps with this (e.g., learning logs).

Agile parallel: Agile retrospectives, where the team reflects on work and generates a concrete list of action steps for the next iteration.

#Example from practice: Onboarding embedded developer (4C Model)

  • C1 - Connections I did not start by immediately explaining the manual for our MCU. I asked him how he had previously solved peripheral initialization and interrupt handling on STM32. I found out that he relied a lot on ST HAL libraries. I drew him a parallel between STM32 HAL and our HW driver layer under the application layer. This immediately connected his previous habits with the way we use HW peripherals in our application.

  • C2 - Concepts I did not give him the task to read hundreds of header files directly in the IDE. We went to the board and drew a high-level block diagram of our middleware. I showed him the sharp boundary where portable code ends, where the hardware adaptation layer is located (which either uses standardized drivers or works directly with registers when extreme optimization is needed) and where the structure of our own HW drivers lies. We discussed the main architectural differences compared to STM32 (engaging multiple senses – visualization, discussion).

  • C3 - Concrete Practice Instead of reading more theory, we took a prototype of our device with a debugging interface and did several pair programming sessions. I selected an isolated small task: get a timer running in the application using the standard cascading timer system provided by the HW layer and connect it to the application layer. I let him write code and search in manuals, I just navigated him (learning by doing). This reduced the gap between the theory from the board and real code.

  • C4 - Conclusions Finally, I had him describe in his own words a summary (teach back) of what is the main difference between using a timer on STM32 and in our context. I had him write down the main findings in his onboarding log and we agreed on another specific task he would work on independently (synchronous LED control on SPI expander). We agreed on a system of quick consultations and daily reviews.
