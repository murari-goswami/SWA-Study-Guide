🟡 Stakeholder presentation

#Marketecture (Marchitecture)

  • What it is

    • Combination of words marketing and architecture.
    • It can be architecture created purely for marketing purposes (highlights strengths, masks weaknesses), but in practice it is more about abstract, non-technical representation of a complex system for discussion with stakeholders (sales, management, customers).
    • According to Ian Gorton, it is "a one-page, typically informal display of system structure, main components and interactions". It serves as a springboard for deeper analysis.
    • It helps unify the product view, plan roadmap and define pricing and packages (packaging).

  • How to build Marketecture

    1. Core buckets: Divide products/system into 3-5 main areas.
    2. Relationships: Map how these areas build on each other (ideally linear progression).
    3. Assignment: Classify specific products/features into these areas.
    4. Core vs. Add-on: Clearly color/visually distinguish what is basic necessary functionality (baked in) and what is optional extension for extra charge (flexible/additive).
    5. Validation: Show it to internal stakeholders and get feedback.
    6. Terminology: Make sure feature names are clear and correspond to positioning (rename if necessary).

#Elevator Pitch

Elevator Pitch

  • The goal is to be able to summarize the main idea of the project and its unique added value (Unique Selling Point - USP) into a short punchy message. It must clearly communicate what problem you solve and in what way your solution exceeds customer expectations.
  • Components:
    • Problem
    • Solution
    • Outcome

#Selling an Architecture

Selling an Architecture

  • Architecture (e.g., pushing through refactoring) is not just a technical task, but a business case. It is about being able to "sell" your vision and get a budget for it.
  • Management is usually not interested in technical details. The key is to show Business Value and justify costs (return on investment, savings of future costs, development acceleration).
  • Before presenting to business, you must prepare specific arguments: economic advantages, risks of current state, strategic alignment with company goals and mapping of alternatives.
  • Tactical advice: Smaller continuous changes are sold to business much easier than requests for money for massive and risky rewrites.

#Presentation for non-technical people

  • What to focus on

    1. Content: Goal, clarity, big picture vs. details, arguments, audience knowledge level.
    2. Behavior: Eye contact, confidence (posture, reading from paper), response to questions, keeping time.
    3. Visualization: Consistency with word, media conflict (listen vs. read), readability.

  • Preparation for live presentation (6-step checklist)

    1. Goals: What do I want? (Inform, persuade, get decision, motivate).
    2. Audience: Who is coming? Identify "critical" people (White knights vs. Black knights), their interests and agenda.
    3. Message: What problem does it solve? Define Unique Selling Point (USP) in one sentence. Map advantages, disadvantages, risks, synergies and alternatives/competition.
    4. Disruptions: What if someone does not come? What if discussion explodes? Prepare for "Killer comments" (e.g., to "This will never work" respond: "What specifically do you think does not work in this concept?").
    5. Roles and resources: Who moderates, who presents, who writes minutes? Does the technology and "look and feel" of slides work?
    6. Personal expression: Active meeting management, avoiding interpersonal conflicts and provocative words, using "I" statements.
